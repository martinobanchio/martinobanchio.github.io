Algorithms Are Setting Prices

Pricing algorithms operate across retail, ad exchanges, ride-sharing, financial markets, rental housing, etc.

Firms increasingly delegate pricing to automated procedures that learn from repeated interaction with competitors doing the same

Growing literature on supracompetitive outcomes:

  • Calvano et al. (2020): Q-learning algorithms sustain collusion in lab
  • Assad et al. (2024): algorithmic pricing markups in gasoline markets
  • ... and many others

What the law says

The DOJ, FTC, and EU Commission are actively investigating algorithmic price-fixing

Regulators' challenge: the law is clear on what constitutes collusion

(Sherman Act §1) Every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy, in restraint of trade or commerce [...] is declared to be illegal.

→ Requires evidence of communication or agreement - parallel pricing alone is not illegal

→ If algorithms learn to coordinate without communicating, can we even call it collusion?

Beyond Price-Setting

Much of the debate has focused on prices

But recent cases brought by the DOJ (AgriStats, RealPage) raise questions about information:

Example: RealPage

  • Landlords adopt RealPage to set rental prices in their apartment buildings
  • DOJ claims granular data about rents and occupancy were pooled and used at runtime
  • Lawsuit settled with prohibitions on use of competitors' information at runtime

Beyond Price-Setting

Remedies forbid RealPage from acting as manager of information cartel

But pricing algorithms may learn to share information implicitly through repeated interaction

Open question: how to detect and prevent such coordination?

Model

The Bayesian Game

A finite Bayesian game $(\Theta, \mu, A, u)$:

  • State space: $\Theta = \Theta_0 \times \Theta_1 \times \cdots \times \Theta_n$
    • $\Theta_0$: payoff-relevant common state - e.g. market conditions
    • $\Theta_i$: private type of player $i$ - e.g. costs
  • Common prior: $\mu \in \Delta(\Theta)$
  • Actions: $A = A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n$ (finite)
  • Payoffs: $u_i: \Theta \times A \to \R$

Timing: Nature draws $\theta \sim \mu$ → player $i$ observes $\theta_i$ → simultaneous actions → payoffs

Both private-value and common-value environments.

Batched Learning

Players' algorithms update decisions over periods $t = 1, 2, \ldots$

Each period, algorithm $i$ selects:

  • Forecasting rule: $\phi_i^t: \Theta_i \to \Delta(\Theta_{-i} \times A_{-i})$
  • Playing rule: $s_i^t: \Theta_i \to A_i$

Key assumption: each period, algorithms interact in many markets
→ the algorithm's empirical distribution of $\theta$ matches the true distribution $\mu$

Analogous to batching in RL: the algorithm accumulates sufficient observations per state before updating — natural when interactions are frequent relative to policy updates

Learning Trajectories

A learning trajectory $(s, \phi)$ is a pair of

  • Playing rules: $s = (\{s_i^t\}_{t \in \mathbb{N}})_i$
  • Forecasting rules: $\phi = (\{\phi_i^t\}_{t \in \mathbb{N}})_i$

A trajectory generates a sequence of empirical distributions $\{\rho^t\}_{t \in \mathbb{N}}$ over $\Theta \times A$:

  • $\rho^t = $ uniform average over pushforward measures induced by $\theta \mapsto (\theta, s^\tau(\theta))$ for $\tau \leq t$

The limit points of $\{\rho^t\}$ describe the long-run outcomes of algorithmic interaction

Algorithms

We impose three requirements on the learning trajectory

1. Calibration

Forecasts are asymptotically correct:

$$\lim_{t \to \infty} \sum_{\varphi_i} \sum_{\theta_i} \sum_{\theta_{-i}, a_{-i}} W^t_i(\varphi_i) \left| \rho^t(\theta_{-i}, a_{-i} \mid \varphi_i, \theta_i) - \varphi_i(\theta_{-i}, a_{-i} \mid \theta_i) \right| \mu_i(\theta_i) = 0$$

where $\rho^t$ is the pushforward of $\mu$ under forecast $\varphi_i$ and $W_i^t(\varphi_i)$ is the empirical frequency of forecast $\varphi_i$ up to time $t$.

Intuitively: over time, what you predicted would happen (conditional on your type and forecast) matches what actually happened

Algorithms

We impose three requirements on the learning trajectory

2. Stationarity

Same forecast ⇒ same action:

$\phi_i^\tau = \phi_i^{\tau'} \;\Rightarrow\; s_i^\tau = s_i^{\tau'}$

where $\rho^t$ is the pushforward of $\mu$ under forecast $\phi_i$ and $W_i^t(\phi_i)$ is the empirical frequency of forecast $\phi_i$ up to time $t$.

Intuitively: players don't randomize arbitrarily. Given the same beliefs, they do the same thing

Algorithms

We impose three requirements on the learning trajectory

3. Rationality

Players always best-respond to their current forecast

$\sum_{\theta_{-i},a_{-i}} \bigg (u_i(\theta,b,a_{-i})-u_i(\theta,s_i^t(\theta_i),a_{-i})\bigg)\phi_i^t(\theta_{-i},a_{-i}\mid\theta_i)\leq 0$

for all $\theta_i \in \Theta_i$ and $b \in A_i$.

Intuitively: algorithms play optimally conditional on their forecast.

Main Results

Convergence

Theorem 1

For all Bayesian games, all limit points $\rho$ of a stationary, calibrated, and rational learning trajectory satisfy:

  • Feasibility: $\rho_\Theta = \mu$  (marginal over states = prior)
  • Obedience: No player can profitably deviate

    $\displaystyle\sum_{\theta, a} u_i(\theta, \alpha_i(\theta_i, a_i), a_{-i}) \rho(\theta, a) \leq \sum_{\theta, a} u_i(\theta, a) \rho(\theta, a) \quad \forall\, \alpha_i$

  • Privacy: Player $i$'s action under $\rho$ depends only on own type

    $\rho(a_i \mid \theta_i, \theta_{-i}) = \rho(a_i \mid \theta_i) \quad \forall\, i, \theta, a_i$

Privately Correlated Equilibrium

Definition

A joint distribution $\rho \in \Delta(\Theta \times A)$ satisfying feasibility, obedience, and privacy is a Privately Correlated Equilibrium (PCE).

Theorem 1 → calibrated, stationary, rational learning converges to PCE

This is a correlated equilibrium in games with incomplete information:

  • One of the five notions of correlated equilibrium for Bayesian games by Forges (1993, 2006)
  • PCE = Belief-Invariant Bayesian Solution (BIBS)
  • First (?) learning foundation for incomplete info correlated equilibrium concept

Aside: Forges' Hierarchy

Concept Information structure Nesting
BCE Arbitrary information structure BS $\subseteq$
BS Bayesian Solution PCE $\subseteq$
BIBS = PCE Belief-invariant Bayesian Solution ← this paper
Agent-normal CE Orthogonal devices only $\subseteq$ PCE
Strategic-form CE Correlated strategies, belief-invariant $\subseteq$ Agent-normal CE
BNE Independent strategies $\subseteq$ Strategic-form CE

Upshot of Theorem 1?

1. Learning foundation for an incomplete information equilibrium concept

  • (Foster and Vohra, 1997) for complete info games

2. The set of PCE is a convex polytope

  • Extreme points maximize linear functionals over the parameters
  • Computation is efficient

3. (mkt. design) Worst-case PCE $\iff$ notion of robustness wrt algorithmic play

  • Parallel to info design (Bergemann and Morris, 2016) for BCE

4. (regulation) Tool for prediction and modeling:

  • Which market structures give rise to gap $PCE \setminus BNE$?
  • Disciplines lawful vs unlawful algorithmic information sharing (Lomys and Magnolfi, 2026)

Applications

Vives (1999)

Canonical environment: differentiated Bertrand with a common demand shifter.

$$D_i(p_i,p_j,\theta)=\theta-p_i+\gamma p_j,\qquad u_i(p_i,p_j,\theta)=p_i\big(\theta-p_i+\gamma p_j\big).$$

  • Each firm gets a private signal $t_i$ about the same shock $\theta$
  • Vives (1999) shows that strategic complements → sharing demand information raises prices
  • Why? Information sharing makes posterior beliefs about demand more volatile
  • In our model → correlation in prices is isolated from belief shifts

The Vives eq. is a communication equilibrium. We restrict to PCE

Solving for PCE

Profit of firm $i$ when recommended $p_i$ → $\mathbb{E}\left[p_i\big(\theta-p_i+\gamma p_j\big) | t_i,p_i \right]$

Obedience → $2p_i=\E[\theta\mid t_i,p_i]+\gamma\,\E[p_j\mid t_i,p_i]$

Belief Invariance → $\E[\theta\mid t_i,p_i]=\E[\theta\mid t_i]=\mu_i(t_i)$

So the FOC becomes:

$$2p_i=\mu_i(t_i)+\gamma\,\E[p_j\mid t_i,p_i]$$

Compare with BNE FOC:

$$2\beta_i(t_i)=\mu_i(t_i)+\gamma\,\E[\beta_j(t_j)\mid t_i]$$

Completing the Proof

Using again belief invariance, we get $\mathbb{E}\left[p_i - \beta_i(t_i) \right]= \frac{\gamma}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[p_j - \beta_j(t_j)\right] = \frac{\gamma^2}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[p_i - \beta_i(t_i)\right]$

Proposition

When $\gamma \neq 2$ there is a unique PCE, and it is the unique BNE:

$$p_i=\beta_i(t_i)\quad\text{a.s.}$$

  • Without posterior shifts, recommendations cannot move prices away from BNE
  • Info sharing in oligopoly requires communication → belongs to BCE, not PCE.

$$PCE \subset BCE$$

Supermodular games

The previous argument is an instance of a more general result for the class of monotone supermodular games.

Theorem 2

In any Bayesian game, every PCE is supported solely on interim correlated rationalizable actions.

If a game is ICR solvable → the PCE is unique (and coincides with the BNE)

Corollary: In symmetric monotone supermodular games, the "lowest" PCE is a BNE

Example: Oligopoly Pricing

Two firms compete across markets $A$ and $B$

  • State $\theta \in \{A,B\}$ (equally likely): which market can support lower prices
  • Alice Inc. privately observes $\theta$;   Bob Ltd. does not
  • Alice chooses which market to cut prices in: $\sigma_a(\theta) \in \{A, B\}$
  • Bob sets a uniform price: $p_H$ (high) or $p_L$ (low)

$\theta = A$

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$10,\;4$$0,\;0$
$B$$8,\;8$$5,\;9$

$a_1 = A$: cuts the right market

$\theta = B$

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$8,\;8$$5,\;9$
$B$$10,\;4$$0,\;0$

$a_1 = B$: cuts the right market

In BNE, Alice exploits its info

Bob cannot condition on $\theta$ → plays a single mixed strategy $q = \P(\sigma_b = p_H)$

Pure-strategy BNEs:

  • Firm 1 cuts the right market, Firm 2 plays $p_H$:   payoffs $(10,4)$  →  industry profit = 14
  • Firm 1 never cuts the right market, Firm 2 plays $p_L$:   payoffs $(5,9)$  →  industry profit = 14

Best mixed BNE:

  • $q = 5/7$,   Alice plays $\sigma_a(\theta) = \theta$ with prob $1/5$

$$\E[\Pi]_{\text{BNE}}^* = \tfrac{50}{7} + \tfrac{36}{5} = \tfrac{502}{35} \approx 14.34$$

PCE: Coordinated Recommendations

An algorithm recommends action pairs $(\sigma_a, \sigma_b)$:

State $\theta = A$:

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$1/4$$0$
$B$$1/2$$1/4$

State $\theta = B$:

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$1/2$$1/4$
$B$$1/4$$0$

Outcome $(8,8)$ occurs with prob $1/2$, Outcome $(0,0)$ never

Note: Bob's recommendation is correlated with the state and with the action of Alice! Yet Bob's posterior about the state is unchanged.

Privacy

State $\theta = A$:

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$1/4$$0$
$B$$1/2$$1/4$

State $\theta = B$:

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$1/2$$1/4$
$B$$1/4$$0$

Bob's marginal recommendation is identical across states:

$$\P(\sigma_b= p_H \mid \theta = A) = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{3}{4} = \P(\sigma_b = p_H \mid \theta = B)$$

→ Bob cannot infer $\theta$ from its recommendation

Privacy is trivially satisfied for Alice.

Privacy

State $\theta = A$:

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$1/4$$0$
$B$$1/2$$1/4$

State $\theta = B$:

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$1/2$$1/4$
$B$$1/4$$0$

Bob's marginal recommendation is identical across states:

$$\P(\sigma_b= p_H \mid \theta = A) = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{3}{4} = \P(\sigma_b = p_H \mid \theta = B)$$

→ Bob cannot infer $\theta$ from its recommendation

Privacy is trivially satisfied for Alice.

Obedience

$\theta = A$

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$10,\;4$$0,\;0$
$B$$8,\;8$$5,\;9$

$\theta = B$

$p_H$$p_L$
$A$$8,\;8$$5,\;9$
$B$$10,\;4$$0,\;0$

In this equilibrium, Bob never cuts prices in the wrong market

Alice's information allows Bob to undercut in the right market, and it simultaneously make it obedient for Alice

  • When Bob is recommended $p_L$:
    • If $\theta = A$, Alice is told to play $B$
        → obedient if $\left(\frac{1}{2}/\frac{3}{4}\right) 8 + \left(\frac{1}{4}/\frac{3}{4}\right) 5 > \left(\frac{1}{2}/\frac{3}{4}\right)10$ ✓
    • Same logic for $\theta=B$

Industry Profit

Profit in this PCE is

$$\frac{1}{2} \left(8+8\right) + \frac{1}{4}\left(10+4\right) + \frac{1}{4}\left(5+9\right) = 15$$

Recall that best BNE → 14.34

Industry profits in PCE $>$ industry profit in any BNE

Connection to Lomys-Magnolfi (JPE R&R)

Structural estimation of algorithmic pricing: do agents run no-regret algorithms?

Lomys-Magnolfi approach:

  • No-regret learning → convergence to $\text{BCCE}$
  • Use BCCE moment inequalities to partially identify cost distributions
  • Wide identified sets (BCCE is permissive)

Our contribution:

  • Add calibration + stationarity → convergence to $\text{PCE}$ (⊊ BCCE)
  • Tighter behavioral restriction on play
  • Narrower identified set for costs and markups

They show *how* to estimate with algorithmic agents; we show *which* solution concept is right for the class of calibrated, rational, stationary algorithms — allowing tighter structural inference.

Proof Sketch: Obedience Step

The substantive step combines all three requirements:

  1. By stationarity, for each forecast $\varphi_i$ there is a unique action $s_i^{\varphi_i}$
  2. Decompose the empirical distribution:

    $\rho^t = \sum_{\varphi_i} W_i^t(\varphi_i) \cdot \rho^t(\cdot \mid \varphi_i)$

  3. By calibration, $\rho^t(\cdot \mid \varphi_i, \theta_i) \approx \varphi_i(\cdot \mid \theta_i)$ as $t \to \infty$
  4. Triangle inequality + uniform bound $H$ on payoff differences:

    gap between payoff at empirical conditional vs. at forecast → 0

  5. By rationality, the payoff gain from deviating under each $\varphi_i$ is $\leq 0$
  6. The limit inherits obedience  $\blacksquare$

Reachability

Theorem 2 (Reachability)

Generically, all privately correlated equilibria are limit points of some calibrated, rational, and stationary learning trajectory.

In particular, this holds for all private-value environments.

Proof strategy:

  • Construct explicit trajectory converging to any given PCE
  • Sequence strategy profiles whose time-average equals the PCE
  • Forecasts: mixture of empirical distribution + vanishing perturbation $(1/(t+1))$
  • Ensures calibration and rationality simultaneously
Takeaway: PCE is the tight characterization of long-run algorithmic outcomes in Bayesian games — nothing more, nothing less

Epistemics and Regulation

Three Levels of Coordination

Our results place PCE precisely between two familiar benchmarks:

BNE — Independent decision-makers

Each player best-responds to the prior. No correlation, no communication.

PCE — Algorithmic correlation

Algorithms condition on shared histories (public prices, past actions). Correlation arises, but no private information is pooled.

BCE — Mediated coordination

A mediator (e.g., RealPage) pools private types and sends correlated recommendations. Requires a centralized information channel.

$$\text{BNE} \;\subseteq\; \color{#C00000}{\text{PCE}} \;\subseteq\; \text{BCE}$$

Epistemic Equivalence

Theorem 3 (Equivalence)

A distribution $\rho$ is a PCE  $\Longleftrightarrow$  it is induced by a BNE of the game augmented with a belief-invariant recommendation scheme.

Belief-invariant scheme $(M, \pi)$:

  • Mediator observes $\theta$, draws messages $m \sim \pi(\cdot \mid \theta)$, sends $m_i$ to player $i$
  • Belief-invariant: $m_i \perp \theta_{-i} \mid \theta_i$ — the message does not reveal opponents' types

Interpretation:

  • PCE = what a constrained mediator can achieve — one that cannot feed pooled private info back to players
  • BCE $\setminus$ PCE = outcomes that require centralized information sharing

Implications for Regulation

The $\text{BNE} \subseteq \text{PCE} \subseteq \text{BCE}$ hierarchy gives regulators a toolkit:

1. Observe market outcome $\rho$

2. Is $\rho \in \text{PCE}$? → Consistent with independent algorithmic learning. Not actionable.

3. Is $\rho \in \text{BCE} \setminus \text{PCE}$? → Requires centralized information pooling. Potentially actionable.

The PCE boundary is a principled bright line: outcomes inside PCE can arise from decentralized algorithms alone; outcomes outside require an identifiable communication channel

Applications

Common-Demand Bertrand: Main Implication

Implication

Every belief-invariant Bayesian solution is outcome-equivalent to the unique BNE:

$$p_i=\beta_i(t_i)\quad\text{a.s.}$$

  • Action correlation is not enough: without posterior shifts, recommendations cannot move prices away from BNE
  • Vives-style price effects require communication: payoff-relevant information must be transmitted
  • Interpretation for your hierarchy: those effects belong to $\text{BCE}\setminus\text{PCE}$, not to decentralized BIBS/PCE learning

Connection to Forges' Five Concepts

Forges (1993, 2006) introduced five notions of correlated equilibrium for Bayesian games, depending on the information structure:

Concept Information structure Relation to PCE
Communication Eq. Arbitrary mediator, full type reports $\supseteq$ PCE
BCE Arbitrary information structure $\supseteq$ PCE
BIBS Belief-invariant schemes = PCE
Agent-normal CE Orthogonal devices only $\subseteq$ PCE
Strategic-form CE Correlated strategies, no types $\subseteq$ PCE

PCE = BIBS (Belief-Invariant Bayesian Solution)

The Privacy condition in PCE is exactly Forges' belief-invariance condition. Our learning-theoretic foundation provides a new justification for BIBS as the natural solution concept for decentralized play.

PCE in the Hierarchy

All inclusions can be strict:

  • Strategic-form CE $\subsetneq$ PCE: there exist PCE that cannot be induced by orthogonal (type-independent) information structures
  • PCE $\subsetneq$ BCE: the mediation gap is generically non-empty whenever there is meaningful private information

Economic implication:

Shared histories give algorithms more power than simple public randomization (strategic-form CE), but less power than a mediator who pools private info (BCE)

When no private information: Privacy is vacuous and PCE = BCE

1.  PCE is the tight learning-theoretic benchmark for Bayesian games
→ Analog of correlated equilibrium for incomplete information
→ Convergence + Reachability ⇒ exact characterization

2.  Epistemic foundation via BIBS

→ Policy-relevant distinction for antitrust enforcement
